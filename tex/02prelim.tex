\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Graph Theory Basics}
In this report, we consider the structure of finite graphs.
Therefore, we go by the convention that a graph $G = (V(G), E(G))$ comprises of a
finite set of vertices $V(G)$ and a set of undirected edges $E(G) \subseteq V(G) \times V(G)$,
meaning that $(i, j) \in E(G)$ iff $(j, i) \in E(G)$.
Throughout, we also use the notation $i \sim_G j$ to mean $(i, j) \in E(G)$.
Furthermore, we omit the graph subscript for this notation, whenever there is no ambiguity.

An independent set of a graph is a subgraph of $G$ containing no edges, 
and the independence number $\alpha(G)$ is defined to be the vertex count
of a maximal independent set of $G$.

A homomorphism of two graphs $G, H$ is defined to be a map $\phi : V(G) \rightarrow V(H)$
such that $i \sim_G j \implies \phi(i) \sim_H \phi(j)$.
Moreover, two graphs $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic if the above implication is two-sided.
An automorphism of a graph $G$ is an isomorphism to itself.

A strongly regular graph with with parameters $(n, k, \lambda, \mu)$ is a graph $G$
with the properties
\begin{itemize}
    \item $|V(G)| = n$,
    \item Each vertex has exactly $k$ neighbours,
    \item Any two adjacent vertices share exactly $\lambda$ neighbours,
    \item Any two non-adjacent vertices share exactly $\mu$ neighbours.
\end{itemize}
Strongly regular graphs have been one central object of study in algebraic graph theory, 
see for example~\cite{Brouwer_Van_Maldeghem_2022}, 
as they lie on the border between structure and disorder and are still poorly understood in general.
In particular, strongly regular graphs are generally not uniquely determined by their parameters,
and for some parameter choices there are exponentially many non-isomorphic graphs,
while for some parameter choices there are none at all.

\subsection{Non-Local Games}
Non-local games consist of two non-communicating players Alice and Bob, which are given 
questions $x \in I_A, y \in I_B$ by the referee, to which they must respond with an answer $a \in O_A, b \in O_B$.
The game is won if the questions and answers fulfill some predefined condition,
captured by a \emph{verification function}
\[ V: I_A \times I_B \times O_A \times O_B~\rightarrow~\{0, 1\}, \]
i.e., if $V(x, y, a, b) = 1$, the game is won.
The probability for the players to receive questions $x$ and $y$ 
are modelled via some probability distribution $\pi: I_A \times I_B \rightarrow [0, 1]$,
which, for simplicity, is generally assumed to be a uniform distribution.

The caveat to non-local games is that the players are not allowed to communicate 
during the game, but are allowed to use entangled \emph{quantum} resources which are distributed before the game.

We therefore distinguish between two types of strategies, namely classical and quantum strategies.
We say that Alice and Bob adopt a (deterministic) \emph{classical strategy} if they agree on functions $f_A: I_A \rightarrow O_A$
and $f_B: I_B \rightarrow O_B$ beforehand, which are applied to their questions during the game.
In particular, no quantum entanglement is used. 
On the other hand, in case of a \emph{quantum strategy}, the players agree to apply local question-dependent 
operations, i.e. unitaries and measurements, onto their share of qudits, 
and answer depending on the result of some measurement on their share of qudits.

The \emph{winning probability} of a non-local game $G$ for some strategy $S$ is defined as
\[ \omega_S(G) = \sum_{x,y} \pi(x, y) \sum_{a,b} V(x,y,a,b)~p(a,b|x,y) \]
where $p(a,b|x,y)$ is the probability for Alice and Bob to respond with $a,b$, given questions $x,y$.
Note that this is the only term that the players
have control over, namely by agreeing on a game strategy $S$ beforehand,
while the other terms are predetermined by the game instance that is played.
In particular, for classical strategies, we may simplify to
\[ p(a,b|x,y) = p\left(f_A(x) = a\right) \cdot p\left(f_B(y) = b\right), \]
while for quantum strategies we may rewrite to
\[ p(a,b|x,y) = \langle \Psi| P_x(a) \otimes P_y(b) |\Psi\rangle, \]
where $P_x$ and $P_y$ are some projections parametrized by the received questions.
It is possible to further generalize to \emph{quantum commuting strategies}, which
allow Alice and Bob to use global measurements $P_x$ on $|\Psi\rangle$,
as long as the measurement operators commute. In this case,
\[ p(a,b|x,y) = \langle \Psi| P_x(a) P_y(b) |\Psi\rangle. \]
It is known that for finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, to which we restrict our attention in this report,
the two notions of quantum and quantum commuting stragegies are equivalent.
%
Studying non-local games is motivated by a peculiar phenomenon called \emph{quantum advantage},
describing that the pre-shared quantum resources allow to devise quantum strategies,
which may achieve higher winning probabilities than classical strategies,
i.e. strategies that do not utilise these quantum resources.
Phrased in terms of the optimal classical (respectively quantum) winning strategy,
\begin{align*}
    \omega^*(G) := \sup_{S \textrm{ classical}}~\omega_S(G), & & \omega_q^*(G) := \sup_{S \textrm{ quantum}} \omega_S(G),
\end{align*}
there exist non-local games $G$ for which $\omega^*(G) < \omega_q^*(G)$.
%
Furthermore, it is known that quantum strategies are non-signalling,
meaning that the answers of the players are statistically independent of each other,
defying intuition that during the game, some form of information is exchanged
via those local operations on the shared entangled pair.


\subsubsection{The Mermin-Peres Magic Square Game.}\label{sec:magic-square}

A prominent example of a non-local game was introduced by Mermin and Peres~\cite{PhysRevLett.65.3373}
and is based on assigning values to a 3 by 3 square.
\begin{definition}
    The \emph{magic square game} is a non-local game based on a 3 by 3 grid with entries $x_i \in \{1, -1\}$,
    such that the product of the $x_i$ over one row must be $1$, while the product over one column must be $-1$.

    The non-local game is then defined as follows.
    The referee asks Alice to fill in some row and Bob to fill in some column.
    The players win if their assignments are valid and if both players assign the same value to
    the element contained in the intersection.  
\end{definition}

Note that it is impossible to find a classical strategy with winning probability one, as it 
would imply filling out the entire magic square, which is impossible by design.
To see this, compare the product of all rows to the product of all columns.
On the one hand, they both compute the product of all entries $\prod_{i = 1}^{9} x_i$ and should
therefore be equal. On the other hand, $1\cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1 \neq -1 = (-1) \cdot (-1) \cdot (-1)$,
giving us the contradiction.


However, it is in fact possible to find an optimal quantum strategy, by sharing two qubit pairs in the Bell state,
i.e. the state 
\[ |\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(|0\rangle |0\rangle + |1\rangle|1\rangle \right)_{A_1 B_1} \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle |0\rangle + |1\rangle|1\rangle \right)_{A_2 B_2}, \]
where $|0\rangle = (1, 0)^T$ and $|1\rangle = (0, 1)^T$ are the standard basis vectors of the Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$,
in which the possible states of a qubit live.
Now, based on the questions they received, Alice and Bob measure their share of qubits via the observables given
in~\ref{fig:quantum-magic-square} and label the square entries with the measurement outcomes,
i.e. with one of the eigenvalues of the observable.
Since the operators in one row or column commute with each other, they share an eigenbasis,
and since quantum measurements translate to projections onto eigenspaces of the observable,
the order of measurement does not affect the outcomes.
Furthermore, as Alice and Bob share two copies of the Bell state, it means that measuring with 
the same observable gives the same outcomes for both players, therefore their answers 
on the intersecting entry always matches. 
Moreover, the product of the eigenvalues inside one row or column is equal to the eigenvalue
of their product, which is by design $I\otimes I$ for the rows and $-I\otimes I$ for the columns,
with eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$ respectively.
Therefore, the assignment produced by the quantum strategy is valid.
Lastly, since no probabilities are involved, we get a quantum strategy with a winning probability of one.

\subsubsection{The Graph Isomorphism Game.}

% After having seen the 
We define another non-local game, which is based on graph isomorphisms.
\begin{definition}
    Let $G, H$ be graphs with the same number of vertices.
    The $(G, H)$-isomorphism game (or graph isomorphism game)
    is the non-local game defined by the following rules:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Alice and Bob both receive vertices $v_A, v_B \in V(G)$
        \item They have to answer with vertices $w_A, w_B \in V(H)$
        \item The game is won if $\textrm{rel}_G(v_A, v_B) = \textrm{rel}_H(w_A, w_B)$,
        where 
        \[ \textrm{rel}_\Gamma:~V(\Gamma) \times V(\Gamma)~\rightarrow~\{ =, \sim, \not \sim \} \]
        denotes the adjacency relation between two vertices of some graph $\Gamma$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
Based on this definition, we further define the notion of a quantum isomorphism between graphs.
\begin{definition}
    Two graphs $G, H$ with the same number of vertices are said to be \emph{quantum isomorphic},
    denoted $G \cong_q H$, if there exists a perfect quantum
    strategy for the $(G, H)$-isomorphism game.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    An immediate observation from those definitions is that the game 
    has an optimal classical strategy when the graphs $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic,
    as applying this isomorphism gives such a strategy.
    Since the quantum strategy of simply ignoring the quantum resources and employing
    this classical strategy is already optimal, we have
    \[ G \cong H \implies G \cong_q H. \]
    Interestingly, the converse is not true.
    As we will see repeatedly, there exist pairs of graphs 
    which are quantum isomorphic yet non-isomorphic.
    These pairs of graphs are the main object of study in this work 
    and to this date, very few examples of these are known.
    Throughout this work, we will simply call such graph pairs 
    \emph{quantum isomorphic} and implictly assume
    that they are non-isomorphic, unless stated otherwise.
\end{remark}
There are many alternative definitions of a quantum isomorphism between graphs,
some of which are more helpful for the explicit construction of those graph pairs.
Moreover, the language describing quantum strategies for the $(G, H)$-graph isomorphism game
is based on $C^*$-algebras, which we define next.
\begin{definition}
    A normed algebra $\mathcal{A}$ is an associative algebra over $\mathbb{C}$
    equipped with a norm $||\cdot ||:\mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$.
    A Banach algebra is a complete normed algebra, i.e. one in which every Cauchy sequence converges.
    Lastly, a $C^*$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ is a Banach algebra with an involution
    $ ^* : \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ such that for any $x, y \in \mathcal{A}$,
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $x^{**} = x$,
        \item $(x+y)^{*} = x^* + y^*$,
        \item $(xy)^{*} = y^* x^*$,
        \item $(\lambda x)^* = \bar{\lambda} x^*$
        \item $||x^* x || = ||x||^2$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    For finite dimensional $\mathbb{C}$ algebras, we may always assume
    \[ \mathcal{A} \cong \bigoplus_{i \in I} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C}) \]
    for some finite multiset $I \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. In particular,
    all $C^*$-algebras appearing in this report are isomorphic to $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ for some
    positive integer $n$, and are therefore unital, the unit being the identity matrix $I_n$.
\end{remark}
Next, we further give a condition for quantum isomorphism based on $C^*$-algebras,
which is stated in more graph theoretic terms.
\begin{definition}\emph{\textbf{\cite{Wang_1998}}}
Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a unital $C^*$-algebra. A \emph{magic unitary}
(or quantum permutation matrix) is a matrix $u \in M_n(\mathcal{A})$
whose entries are projections in $\mathcal{A}$, i.e.
$\forall i,j \in \{1,\dots, n\}:~u_{ij} = u_{ij}^* = u_{ij}^2$, and furthermore
\begin{align}
    \forall i \in \{1,\dots, n\}:~\sum_k u_{ik} = \sum_k u_{ki} = \mathbf{1}_\mathcal{A}
\end{align}
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    Note that this definition generalizes the notion of
    a permutation matrix, since the definitions coincide for 
    the special case $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{C}$
    (here the set of projections is just $\{0,1 \}$).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
    Let $G, H$ be graphs. $G$ and $H$ are quantum isomorphic iff
    there exists a unital $C^*$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ and
    a magic unitary $u \in M_n(\mathcal{A})$ such that 
    \[ A_G u = u A_H, \]
    where $A_G$ and $A_H$ are the adjacency matrices of $G$ and $H$ respectively.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    Continuing the previous remark, we see that the special case 
    $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{C}$ translates to quantum isomorphisms
    being equivalent to classical isomorphisms, as the adjacency
    matrices of classically isomorphic graphs are conjugate
    under some permutation, represented by a permutation matrix.
    Moreover, the unital $C^*$-algebras appearing in our discussion about
    quantum isomorphic graph pairs will all be of the form
    $\mathcal{A} = M_n(\mathbb{C})$ for some $n > 1$.
\end{remark}
The condition that $u$ interchanges the adjacency matrices of $G$ and $H$ may 
be reformulated in terms of products of the entries of $u$, which gives a more
concrete approach to showing the quantum isomorphism induced by $u$.
\begin{lemma}~\cite{schmidt2022quantumisomorphicstronglyregular}\label{lem:prod2}
    $G$ and $H$ are quantum isomorphic if there exists a magic unitary $u \in M_n(\mathcal{A})$,
    for which $u_{ks} u_{lt} = 0$ for $(k, l) \in E(G)$ and $(s, t) \not \in E(H)$, or vice versa.
\end{lemma}

\subsubsection{The $(G, t)$-independent set game~\cite{Man_inska_2023}.}\label{def:quindep}
Continuing with non-local games related to graphs, we also define one which relates
to the graph property of containing a $t$-independent set.
\begin{definition}
    Let $t$ be a natural number and $G$ be a graph. Then, the \emph{$(G, t)$-independent set game}
    is defined as follows.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Alice and Bob receive natural numbers $1 \leq a, b \leq t$
        \item They answer with vertices $x_a, x_b \in V(G)$
        \item The game is won if $a = b \implies x_a = x_b$ and $a \neq b \implies x_a \not\sim x_b$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

More generally, we define the notion of a quantum independent set of a graph $G$, as projections 
that yield an optimal quantum strategy for the $(G, t)$-independent set game.
\begin{definition}
    Let $G$ be a graph and $\mathcal{A}$ a unital $C^*$-algebra.
    A \emph{quantum independent set} of size $t$ is a collection
    $P = \{ P_{xu} \}_{1 \leq x \leq t, u \in V(G)}$ of projections
    $P_{xu} \in \mathcal{A}$ such that
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\sum_{u \in V(G)} P_{xu} = \mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}}$ for all $1 \leq x \leq t$
        \item $P_{xu} P_{yv} = 0$ for $(u, v) \in E(G)$ and all $1 \leq x,y \leq t$
        \item $P_{xu} P_{yu} = 0$ for all $x \neq y, u \in V(G)$
    \end{itemize}
    Furthermore, the \emph{quantum independence number} $\alpha_q(G)$ is defined as the maximal
    size of a quantum independent set of $G$.
\end{definition}
By construction, the game has an optimal classical strategy if $G$ contains an independent set of size $t$,
as the strategy then involves labelling the independent set as $x_1, \dots, x_t$, giving an optimal strategy.
However, as shown in~\cite{Man_inska_2023}, optimal quantum strategies do not imply
the existence of a $t$-independent set in $G$, meaning that there exist graphs $G$ for which
the classical independence number $\alpha(G)$ is strictly smaller than $\alpha_q(G)$.
